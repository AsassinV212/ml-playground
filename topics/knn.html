The algorithm

To predict the class of a new point:

  1. measure the distance from it to every known point;
  2. take the k closest ones;
  3. answer with the class that most of them have.

That's all. There is nothing to train: the "model" is the data itself. The price is paid at prediction time, when every query has to look through the whole dataset. Choose Probe and drag around to see the neighbours being asked.

A physics picture

With k = 1 every point owns the region of space that is closer to it than to any other point. These are Voronoi cells; in a crystal the same construction gives the Wigner–Seitz cell. The map becomes a mosaic of tiny territories, and one mislabelled point carves out its own island.

Larger k is coarse-graining: averaging over a bigger volume, like measuring temperature with a bigger thermometer. Noise is averaged away, but so are fine details. With k equal to the number of points, the whole map votes for the most common class.

Choosing k honestly

With k = 1 the training accuracy is always 100%: each point is its own nearest neighbour. That number is useless. Leave-one-out accuracy is fair: each point is classified by all the other points, as if it were new data. Press Noisy and move k: training accuracy falls while leave-one-out accuracy first rises, then falls. The best k is somewhere in the middle.

The distance matters

"Nearest" depends on how distance is measured. The set of points within a fixed distance is a circle for the Euclidean distance and a diamond for the Manhattan distance |dx| + |dy|. Probe a point and switch the metric to see the neighbourhood change shape. In real data, features measured in different units (metres and kilograms) must be scaled first, or one of them will dominate the distance.
