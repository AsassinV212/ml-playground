What is gradient descent?

Training a model means finding parameters that make the loss (the error) small. Picture the loss as a landscape: every point on the map is one choice of parameters (x, y), and the height there is the loss. We want the lowest valley.

The gradient ∇L is an arrow pointing in the direction where the loss grows fastest; its length says how steep the slope is. So we step the opposite way:

p_new = p − learning_rate × ∇L(p)

Repeat this and the point slides downhill until the ground is flat (the gradient is almost zero).

A physics picture

Think of a ball rolling in thick honey. Friction is so strong that the ball has no momentum: its velocity is simply proportional to the force, and the force is −∇L. Gradient descent simulates exactly this motion, and the learning rate is the size of the time step in that simulation.

Too small a learning rate

Tiny time steps: the path is smooth and safe, but it takes hundreds of steps to get anywhere. On a real model each step costs a pass over the data, so this wastes a lot of time.

Too large a learning rate

Big time steps overshoot. The ball jumps past the bottom to the opposite slope, which is even steeper, so the next jump is bigger still. On the simple bowl each step multiplies the distance to the minimum by (1 − 2 × learning_rate):

  • below 0.5 — the ball moves straight to the bottom;
  • between 0.5 and 1 — it bounces from side to side but still settles;
  • above 1 — every bounce is bigger than the last, and the loss explodes.

In the stretched valley the steep direction sets the limit, so with a large learning rate the ball zig-zags across the valley while creeping slowly along it. Fixing this is what methods like momentum and Adam are for.

Local minima

Gradient descent only sees the slope under its feet. On the third surface, start on the right and the ball settles in the shallow dip; start on the left and it finds the deeper one. Where you start matters.
